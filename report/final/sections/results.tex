\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Scores are mSCF1 at the matched budget $K_{\text{bin}}$, at bin level, with
differences paired by section. The two core VAE arms are given as
mean $\pm$ SD over three training seeds, one-seed results are marked as such, and the statistics of
\cref{sec:stats} are reported beside the outcome labels.

\subsection{Baseline reproductions}
\label{sec:res-repro}
Each baseline was first run under its own protocol and evaluator. msiPL
reproduced closely on all three collections: 0.414 against 0.403 published on
GBM, 0.402 against 0.431 on CAC and 0.157 against 0.159 on RCC.
With S\textsuperscript{3}PL's own evaluator, the CAC mean was 0.547
$\pm$ 0.038 over ten seeds against 0.601 published. The published value lies
just outside our seed distribution, and the predeclared conclusion is ``not
explained by seed selection alone''.
RCC reached 0.41--0.44 over three seeds against 0.47 published, while GBM
reached 0.319 $\pm$ 0.042 against 0.496 (\cref{sec:res-s3pl}).

\subsection{Ranking by correlation outperforms IG (RQ1, RQ2)}
\label{sec:res-main}
\Cref{tab:main} scores every ranking on the same segmentation and budget.

\textbf{Against legacy msiPL (RQ1).} Both VAE--IG arms beat reproduced msiPL in
every GBM and CAC section: 0.518 and 0.514 against 0.414 on GBM, and 0.530 and
0.553 against 0.402 on CAC (\cref{tab:main}). The predeclared tests, run on
one seed, are statistically supported in all four comparisons, and the
seed-averaged values agree (HL $+0.10$ to $+0.15$, $r=1$, Holm $p=0.031$).
Repeating the comparison with msiPL re-tuned to $0.5\times$--$2\times
K_{\text{bin}}$, IG stayed ahead in all eight GBM sections at every budget.
On CAC it stayed ahead from $K_{\text{bin}}$ upwards, but at half the budget msiPL
was about as good (IG ahead in 5/8 sections), so the predeclared requirement
that the advantage hold at every budget was met on GBM but not on CAC.
On RCC, where msiPL cannot produce lists as long as $K_{\text{bin}}$ in six of
eight runs, IG beat msiPL in all eight runs when both were scored at msiPL's
own list length (0.37 against 0.16).
This compares complete pipelines and does not show which ingredient causes the
gain.

\textbf{IG against posterior correlation (RQ2).} Holding each fitted GMM fixed,
ranking bins by their correlation with the posterior maps outperformed IG in
140 of 144 comparisons (8 sections $\times$ 3 collections $\times$ 2 arms
$\times$ 3 seeds; \cref{tab:seeds,fig:igcorr}). The four exceptions were small,
at most $+0.017$. The predeclared rule asked whether IG was below correlation in
at least 7 of 8 sections, with a mean difference of at most $-0.02$, in every
training run, at each applicable scoring level. It held in 16 of 18 runs; it failed for one CAC training seed in both
arms (IG below in 6 of 8 sections at peak level for the centre-only arm, and at
both levels for the uniform arm), so the rule's verdict is that the result
``does not hold overall''. The direction itself
never reversed: all 30 collection means of IG minus correlation (18 at bin
level and 12 at peak level) were negative.

\textbf{Statistics.} Separately from that replication rule, with seeds
averaged within section, the difference is statistically supported (Holm
$p<0.05$ with the same direction as the outcome) in all six collection--arm
comparisons at bin level (\cref{tab:seeds}). These p-values are nominal,
because CAC sections share one tumour and GBM sections share PDX lines; the
GBM result also holds in each of the four lines. Correlation scored higher in every section (rank-biserial
$r=-1$), and every raw p-value is the smallest attainable for $n=8$, so this is
as strong as eight sections can show. The seed changed the size of the gap
only slightly: the three seed means of a collection differed by at most 0.05.
At peak level (P1) the result is also supported, except for the CAC uniform arm
($-0.054$, 7/8, Holm $p=0.078$). GBM is negative in every PDX line.
On the two development sections, sampling 48 or 192 pixels per component
for IG instead of 12 raised its score by at most 0.024, far less than the gap.

\textbf{Answer to RQ1 and RQ2.} The pipeline beats legacy msiPL, but the
attribution method is not what carries that gain. Given the same segmentation,
a direct correlation ranking is better than IG on all three collections when
$K$ matches the annotated classes, and on CAC and RCC also when $K$ is chosen
without labels (\cref{sec:res-lfk}). The useful signal is in the segmentation,
not in the gradients, and on GBM it depends on that segmentation matching the
tissue classes.

\subsection{Simple rankings and the metric}
\label{sec:res-simple}
Among the unsupervised rankings, the strongest differs by collection
(\cref{tab:main}): posterior correlation on GBM (0.762, with Moran's~$I$ at
0.684), mean intensity on CAC (0.612, with correlation, Moran's~$I$ and
variance all within 0.02) and Moran's~$I$ on RCC (0.631, above correlation at
0.54--0.56). CAC is close to saturation at its budget: all methods, including
the oracle, converge near 0.41 at twice the budget, which limits how much CAC
differences can reveal. On GBM and RCC, sections whose GMM agrees better with
the masks have correlation scores closer to the oracle (Spearman $\rho=-0.90$
and $-0.84$ over the 16 section--arms of one training seed, between balanced accuracy and the gap to the
oracle). mSCF1 rewards ion images that
follow the expert regions, so a segmentation that resembles those regions
scores well when correlated against; this alignment is part of why correlation
does well (\cref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Spatial context in the encoder and the segmentation (RQ3)}
\label{sec:res-spatial}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Uniform $3\times3$ context} helps IG on CAC ($+0.023$ at bin
        level and $+0.027$ at peak level over three seeds, gaining in 8/8
        sections; per seed: helps, mixed, equivalent), although this is not statistically supported (Holm
        $p=0.13$), and it gives no gain under correlation ranking (0.000). On
        GBM and RCC, context and centre-only are equivalent ($-0.004$, 3/8
        and $+0.018$, 6/8). Across the four GBM PDX lines, context is
        slightly positive in one line ($+0.016$) and negative in three.
  \item \textbf{Spatial specificity.} If the CAC gain came from correct local
        placement, real neighbours should beat neighbours shuffled from
        distant pixels. They tied (0.542 against 0.543). On GBM, zeroed
        context scored highest (0.459, against 0.446 for real). Both
        checks used IG, one training seed and the seven confirmation sections
        of each collection.
  \item \textbf{Larger windows} did not help: $7\times7$ and $9\times9$ were
        $0.035$ below $3\times3$ on GBM (mixed; higher in only 2/8 sections)
        and equivalent on CAC (at peak level $-0.018$, higher in 1/8 sections
        for $7\times7$; \cref{fig:variants}).
  \item \textbf{Learned attention} was equivalent to uniform averaging on both
        collections ($+0.011$ GBM, $-0.006$ CAC). With real neighbours its
        weights stayed almost uniform (entropy divided by its maximum,
        $\log$ of the number of neighbours: 0.98--0.999). With
        shuffled neighbours it learned selective weights, so the mechanism
        works; real neighbours are simply near-copies of the centre (mean
        cosine similarity between centre and neighbour spectra 0.97--0.98). At tissue boundaries, where neighbours differ most,
        attention did depart further from uniform on the two development
        sections (median half-$L_1$ distance from equal weights 0.011--0.028
        against 0.003--0.011 in the interior),
        but it favoured neighbours of the same tissue class in only 50--56\%
        of boundary pixels on CAC and 41--50\% on GBM, so it did not learn to
        follow tissue boundaries. Masking the centre during
        training did not make attention selective, and it lowered GBM scores
        by $0.039$. On the two development sections, replacing
        learned attention weights with equal weights at inference slightly
        raised peak scores ($+0.0099$ and $+0.0038$); this same-checkpoint
        diagnostic did not retrain a uniform model.
  \item \textbf{Spatially smoothed segmentation} never helped: four
        comparisons were equivalent and two were mixed, GBM uniform ($+0.029$,
        driven by two sections) and RCC uniform ($-0.021$, 4/8).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Answer to RQ3.} Neighbourhood information entering the encoder or the
segmentation gave no consistent, spatially specific gain in this pipeline. The
spatial information that did help sits in the ranking statistic: correlation
with spatially coherent segment maps, and Moran's~$I$, which ranks bins by how
spatially structured their ion images are.

\subsection{Is the nonlinear encoder needed? (RQ2)}
\label{sec:res-encoder}
Replacing the VAE latent space with a 5-component PCA, while keeping the GMM and
correlation ranking, gave VAE $-$ PCA-5 differences, averaged over three seeds,
of $+0.042$ on GBM (higher in 5/8 sections), $-0.012$ on CAC and $-0.010$ on
RCC. The predeclared outcomes were mixed on GBM and CAC
and equivalent on RCC, and seed averaging did not change them. VAE segmentations matched the masks slightly
better (balanced accuracy 0.868 against 0.839 on GBM) without reliably better
peaks. Reconstruction did not separate the two either: PCA-5, fitted once per
section, had \emph{lower} reconstruction error (mean squared error on
TIC-normalised spectra) than the one-seed centre-only VAE on all eight GBM sections, and slightly
higher error on 7/8 CAC sections. The choice of the number of GMM components is examined in
\cref{sec:res-lfk}.

\textbf{Training length.} On two development sections (GBM108\_positive and
40TopL), both core arms were also scored after 10, 25, 50 and 100 epochs. The
training loss fell mostly by epoch 25 and changed little afterwards (on GBM,
from 593{,}007 to 575{,}894 and then to 571{,}502 at epoch 100). mSCF1 did not
improve with longer training: across the four checkpoints IG varied by at most
0.04 and posterior correlation by at most 0.03, with no consistent trend.
Epoch 100 reproduced the production scores and rankings exactly, so the
100-epoch setting was not chosen on the score.


\subsection{S\textsuperscript{3}PL: neighbour dependence, reproduction and seeds}
\label{sec:res-s3pl}
\textbf{Neighbour dependence.} Retraining S\textsuperscript{3}PL with each patch
filled by copies of the centre spectrum collapsed it on CAC from 0.591 to
0.15--0.18 (8/8 sections). Neighbours shuffled from distant pixels recovered
most of the score (0.55), and correct placement added about $+0.04$, but
inconsistently (mixed, Holm $p=0.63$). S\textsuperscript{3}PL therefore needs
neighbour input under its patch objective, but a consistent benefit from
\emph{correctly placed} neighbours was not established. Training for longer
made it worse (CAC 0.591 to 0.336 and GBM 0.316 to 0.212 from 10 to 100
epochs; epoch 10 was best in 15 of 16 sections). The released 10-epoch
protocol therefore acts as an effective early-stopping choice, but these
scores provide no label-free rule for selecting that checkpoint.

\textbf{Reproduction gap and seeds.} Over ten released seeds, scored
with S\textsuperscript{3}PL's own evaluator, the published GBM score of 0.496 lies above all ten
seed means (maximum 0.374) and above every seed in 6/8 sections; even the best
of ten seeds in every section reaches only 0.434. On CAC, the best of ten
reaches 0.600, just below the published 0.601. Replacing the released
spatial-maximum normalisation with the TIC normalisation described in the paper
did not close the GBM gap (0.303 against 0.324). We therefore leave the GBM gap
unexplained; untested candidates are section-specific data preparation and
hyperparameters tuned on the evaluation sections. The seed effect itself is
specific: about half of the score variance lies between sections, almost none
in a seed main effect and about 47\% in the residual (section $\times$ seed interaction plus run
noise).
Seeds switch particular sections between distinct solutions (GBM108\_positive
between 0.02 and 0.46). On three sections that switched in this way, weight
initialisation dominated in two (SD 0.222 against 0.001 from data order on
GBM108\_positive), while data order also mattered on GBM108\_negative. The
measured contribution of GPU nondeterminism was limited in the tested repeats.


\textbf{Summary.} msiPL reproduced. S\textsuperscript{3}PL's own results were
close on RCC and CAC, with the published CAC value just outside our seed
distribution, but its published GBM value remains unreproduced. Individual sections can
switch between distinct solutions, making single-run comparisons unreliable.

\subsection{Number of segments chosen without labels}
\label{sec:res-lfk}
Because the GMM's $K$ equals the number of annotated classes, we repeated the
ranking comparison with $K$ chosen without labels. BIC selected $K=6$ in all 48
section--arms, the largest value tested ($K\le6$), so this is BIC's choice at
the boundary of the range rather than a clear optimum. Using the existing
models (one seed), we refitted the GMM at $K=6$ and recomputed IG and posterior
correlation; all IG runs passed the completeness check (one at 256 steps), and
the rankings that do not depend on $K$ reproduced their earlier scores exactly.
The predeclared rule asked whether correlation still beat IG in at least 7 of
8 sections (\cref{tab:lfk}).

and IG became about equal. The change came almost entirely from correlation:

\subsection{RCC: held-out confirmation}
\label{sec:res-rcc}
On the held-out RCC collection, correlation outperformed IG in 23 of 24
centre-only and 24 of 24 uniform comparisons over three seeds, and Moran's~$I$ was the strongest unsupervised ranking (0.63). The primary
GMM used class-informed $K=3$. The unannotated class covers 45--78\% of pixels;
the Cardinal workflow treats it as background, but we did not verify that
interpretation independently. The documentation describes
heterogeneous tumour with large normal-looking areas in UH0505\_12 and
UH9905\_18. When only cancer and normal
pixels are scored (one training seed), which changes the question to separating cancer from
normal tissue only, the advantage shrinks: correlation beats IG in 5/8 runs for
the centre-only arm (and falls below mean intensity, variance and Moran's~$I$)
and in 6/8 runs for the uniform arm. The three-class RCC result may therefore
partly reflect separating annotated tissue from the unannotated region;
RCC is held-out confirmation of the ranking contrast under these qualifications.

\subsection{Runtime}
\label{sec:res-runtime}
\Cref{tab:runtime} gives median times per section. Once a segmentation
exists, correlation ranking takes about a second or less, while the IG step
takes seconds on CAC and minutes on GBM and RCC. The two differ in scope:
correlation starts from loaded spectra and a fitted GMM, whereas the IG step
also fits the GMM and runs completeness checks. Every timed ranking reproduced
its saved ranking exactly. The 100-epoch VAE trains for longer than
S\textsuperscript{3}PL's 10-epoch protocol, but over a complete workflow on CAC
section 160TopL (training, selection and scoring, three repeats;
\cref{fig:runtime}) the VAE took 122--135\,s against 151--174\,s for
S\textsuperscript{3}PL, with 1.2 against 1.3\,GiB of host memory and 237
against 315\,MiB of GPU memory. These are measurements under each method's own
protocol, not an equal-work comparison.
